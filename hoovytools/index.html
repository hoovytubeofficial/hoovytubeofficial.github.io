HoovyTools: SFM to Blender

⬇ Download HoovyTools (.zip) Get SourceIO

Developed between 2023 and 2026 by HoovyTube with the permission of RedEye (creator of SourceIO), HoovyTools brings full SFM session import into Blender for the first time: shape keys, cameras, sounds and animations, in harmony with SourceIO.

Documentation starts from this release. Older versions, and builds targeting other Blender versions, will be documented here over time.

Installing HoovyTools

HoovyTools runs on top of SourceIO. SourceIO does the heavy lifting of loading Source engine models, materials and textures into Blender; HoovyTools sits on top and rebuilds the SFM session around them. Install SourceIO first, then HoovyTools.

  1. Install SourceIO: Edit → Preferences → Add-ons → Install…, choose its zip, and enable it.
  2. Download the HoovyTools .zip above. Do not unzip it.
  3. Back in Add-ons → Install…, choose the HoovyTools zip, then tick the checkbox to enable it.
  4. Open the sidebar in the 3D viewport (press N). The HoovyTools panel lives there.

Wiring in your usermod

SFM keeps your custom models, materials and sounds inside your usermod (and the game folders it mounts). HoovyTools resolves every asset a session references by those same relative paths, so it needs to know where your content lives. Point HoovyTools at your SFM usermod (and any mounted game folders) and it finds your models, textures and sounds exactly the way SFM did, with nothing renamed or re-pathed. If a model comes in missing, it is almost always a usermod path that has not been added yet.

How it works, feature by feature

Point HoovyTools at an SFM session (a .dmx file) and it rebuilds that session inside Blender in one pass. Here is everything the current release brings across:

  • SFM session import. Reads the whole session (shots, clips and the timeline) and reconstructs it in one go.
  • Animations. Every bone channel is sampled and baked onto the Blender armature, keyframe for keyframe.
  • Shape keys. Facial flexes and flex controllers come across as native Blender shape keys, animated over the timeline.
  • Cameras. Your SFM camera is rebuilt as a Blender camera with position, rotation and FOV, keyed exactly as it moved.
  • Sounds. The Session Sounds Auto-Importer places every voice line and sound from the session onto the timeline, on the frame.
  • FPS-aware timing. Everything is retimed to your scene (for example 24 → 30) so animation, sound and camera stay locked together. No manual re-timing.
  • Non-destructive. Assets keep their original paths and names, so a re-import lands in the same place.

Under the hood: how the import actually works

None of this is magic, and it is worth explaining on a human level. An SFM session is a DMX file, a tree of elements that describes your shot. HoovyTools reads it, walks that tree, and translates each piece into the closest thing Blender understands.

Parsing the session

The first job is turning the raw session into data we can work with. HoovyTools reads the DMX directly (the same format datamodel / dmxconvert operate on), decoding the binary or keyvalues payload into an in-memory tree. From there it does not guess: it looks up the session, its clips, and the specific channels attached to each element.

What we actually parse

  • SESSION data: the clip and shot structure, plus the master timeline everything else hangs off.
  • ANIMATION data: per-bone transform channels, sampled and baked onto the armature.
  • CAMERA data: the camera transform and FOV channels.
  • Sound clips: the game sounds a session references and their frame positions.
  • Flex / shape channels: the flex-controller values that drive faces.

Fifteen small scripts, stacked

Rather than one giant importer, HoovyTools is really about fifteen small, single-purpose scripts stacked on top of each other. Each does exactly one thing and hands clean data to the next, which is what makes a full session import feel seamless instead of fragile.

01DMX reader opens the session and decodes it into a tree.
02Session walker finds the shots, clips and the timeline.
03Model resolver hands model references to SourceIO to load.
04Animation baker samples bone channels and keys the armature.
05Shape-key mapper turns flex controllers into shape keys.
06Camera mapper rebuilds the camera transform and FOV.
07Sound placer drops each sound onto the timeline, on the frame.
08Timing / FPS converter retimes every channel to the scene.
…and the rest: coordinate-space fixes, unit conversion, naming, cleanup and validation.

Where Blender stands in for SFM

SFM concepts do not always exist one-to-one in Blender, so HoovyTools maps each to the nearest native construct:

  • Flex controllers → Blender shape keys.
  • SFM camera → Blender camera with animated F-Curves.
  • DMX sound clips → sound strips on the timeline, on the frame.
  • SFM game units and coordinate space → Blender's units and axes.
  • Session timeline → Blender's scene frame range and FPS.

The result: your SFM work lands in a modern, far more stable environment, without you rebuilding a single thing by hand.

HoovyTools Particle Import

⬇ Download Particle Import (.zip)

The problem this solves

Everything else in an SFM project can leave SFM. Models, maps, animation, cameras - between SourceIO, Blender Source Tools and the session importer above, all of it has a road into Blender. The particles never did. An SFM effect is a .pcf file: a description of emitters, forces and sprites that only Source itself knew how to play. Move your scene to Blender and every explosion, every muzzle flash, every trail of smoke simply stays behind - and you are left re-animating fire by hand, or compositing it in from stock footage that never quite sits in the scene.

Particle Import reads the .pcf itself and rebuilds the whole system inside Blender as geometry nodes - not a baked point cloud, not a video, but the living machine: emitters that emit, forces that push, sprites that face the camera and play their sheets. Your effect arrives editable, with every parameter the original author tuned sitting in a panel, ready to be retuned.

Standing on very tall shoulders

This addon exists because two others existed first. Blender Source Tools taught a generation of us that Source assets and Blender could talk at all, and SourceIO turned that conversation into something effortless. I am a fan of both, in the plainest sense of the word - someone who spent years inside SFM leaning on tools other people were kind enough to build and give away.

Particle Import is written in that same spirit and stands, happily, in their shadow: it takes on the one asset type neither of them covers, so the road out of SFM finally has no missing lane. No grand claims - if it helps a few SFM folks keep their effects when they move, it has done its job.

Battle-tested where it counts: I ran it against the hundreds of particle systems I have built for my supporters over the years - the weirdest, heaviest effects I could throw at it - and nearly all of them import and play correctly.

Installing it

Particle Import is standalone. It reads .pcf, .vtf and .vmt files directly - no SourceIO required, no VTFEdit, no converters of any kind.

  1. Download the .zip above. Do not unzip it.
  2. Edit → Preferences → Add-ons → Install…, choose the zip, tick the checkbox to enable it.
  3. Open the sidebar in the 3D viewport (press N) and find the Particle Import tab.
  4. It auto-detects your SFM install; if yours lives somewhere unusual, set the paths in the addon preferences.
  5. Pick a .pcf, pick a system, press Build. Press play.

What comes across

  • The whole system, live. Emitters, initializers, per-frame operators, forces and constraints - rebuilt as geometry nodes, not baked.
  • Sprites, ropes and trails. The three big Valve renderers: camera-facing animated sprites, beam ropes, and velocity-stretched spark trails.
  • Textures decoded in-house. .vtf sprite sheets and .vmt materials are read directly - $additive, $overbrightfactor and sheet sequences included.
  • Control points as empties. Effects that follow points in SFM follow empties in Blender. Grab one and drag it; the effect comes along.
  • Everything editable, live. Every parameter the .pcf authored appears in a properties panel, organised by SFM's own sections - and edits apply to particles already in the air.
  • Sharing built in. Node to Code turns a system you have built into a script anyone can run - no addon needed on their end.
  • Performance tools. Proxy View swaps heavy effects for cheap colour-coded stand-ins while you work, and a lag-diagnosis button tells you exactly what is slow instead of leaving you guessing.

Under the hood: the geometry-node machine

Tab into any imported system and you will not find node soup. You will find a machine organised the way Source itself thinks about particles, colour-coded by job:

Time - the clock. SFM runs at 24 FPS internally; a time-scale group retimes it to your scene.
Emitter - decides when particles are born, and how many.
Initializers - decisions made once, at birth: starting position, radius, colour, lifetime.
Operators - decisions remade every single frame: movement, fading, spinning, forces.
Sequence maths - which tile of the sprite sheet each particle shows right now.
Renderer - turns all of the above into camera-facing cards your GPU can draw.

That is Valve's own anatomy, kept on purpose: if you have ever used SFM's particle editor, you already know your way around the graph.

The emitter is fussy about debt

Thirty times a second, the emitter asks how many particles it owes the world. If the rate says two and a half, it spawns two and carries the half forward - rounding would slowly drift the effect away from what its author tuned. And when the population cap is reached, the cap throttles rather than kills: emission pauses until a particle dies naturally. Deleting a living particle to make room is a bug, not a feature.

The emitter remembers; the operators recompute

The simulation zone - Blender's frame-to-frame memory - stores exactly four things per particle: its ID, its birth time, its birth position, and one flag. Everything else, every frame, is recomputed as a pure function of that ID. This is the design decision the whole addon hangs off, and it buys you something you can feel: grab Radius while the effect is playing and every particle already in the air resizes instantly. Nothing is baked, so nothing needs re-baking.

Randomness without dice

If everything recomputes each frame, how does "random" not flicker? Because there are no dice - there is a hash: fract(sin(id × 12.9898 + 78.233) × 43758.5453). Feed a particle's ID through it and you get a number that looks random but is utterly repeatable. Particle #47 rolls the same size every time you ask, forever - which is exactly why you can scrub the timeline backwards and the effect holds together.

Valve's physics, on Valve's clock

Source applies drag as a fraction of velocity kept per tick, thirty ticks a second - not per second. The graphs honour that, down to terminal velocity being gravity ÷ (30 × drag). One Blender unit is one Hammer unit, and nothing is secretly rescaled on the way in. It is the difference between "looks kind of like TF2" and an effect a Source character could stand next to without anything reading wrong.

Cards, and the art of facing the camera

Every particle you have ever seen in Source is a flat card turning to face you - but how it turns is authored, and the renderer reproduces all three modes. Smoke billboards fully. Fire uses the erect mode: it follows you as you orbit but stays standing, and it refuses random roll, because a flame doing a cartwheel breaks the illusion instantly. The third mode locks to a control point's rotation. Mirror-flips are done the honest way - the card's X scale goes negative - and the whole sheet animation runs through one material, with each particle privately choosing its sequence through a per-point attribute the shader reads at instance level.

The graph explains itself

Finally, the part I am proudest of: every section of every generated graph carries a written note - inside the node editor, in a coloured frame - saying what that section does, how, and what the trap was. You can learn how the machine works by reading the machine. No wiki required.
